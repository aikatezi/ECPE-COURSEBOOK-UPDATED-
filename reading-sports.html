TEXT 01

QUIET EYE

Improving performance in sports

READING AUDIO — QUIET EYE
The factors that contribute to successful performance in sport are many and complex. Scientists, however, have pinpointed one phenomenon, identified as Quiet Eye (QE), which seems to be associated with peak performance not only in sports, but also when performing surgery, flying a jet, or doing anything that requires hand-eye coordination. QE is the intense concentration of the eyes on a task-relevant location just before the final phase of a desired movement. The eye only needs to remain still for a fraction longer to make a difference, and empirical studies have shown that the discrepancy in focus duration between an amateur and an expert may be as little as one-fifth of a second. While there is some overlap between them, quiet eye differs significantly from the state called the zone. When an athlete enters the zone, he or she becomes completely absorbed in the task at hand, and immune to all distractions. During this much sought-after state, limitations fade away and pure intuition seems to take over. Outstanding athletes describe the zone as an almost mystical experience during which there is an impression of perfect harmony between mind and body. Quiet eye, on the other hand, refers specifically to visual perception. It is a measurable phenomenon, and crucially, it is teachable. One study of basketball players found that a team trained in QE techniques over two seasons improved their free-throw accuracy by 23% and attained a total 76% success rate. Teams without the training improved to a much lesser extent, if at all. Interestingly, children with mobility or coordination issues, whose self-consciousness may prevent them from playing with other children, also respond well to this type of training. In an experiment involving 30 children, those who underwent QE training that involved tracking a tennis ball with their eyes had much better outcomes than those trained only in physical techniques. Such improvements may keep children's anxiety at bay when playing with others. In light of these discoveries, devotion solely to honing physical skills, which has taken priority in much sports and professional training, may now shift to including quiet eye training in order to reach higher levels of success.

TEXT 02

TECH
DOPING

The use of technology in sports

READING AUDIO — TECH DOPING
For competitive athletes, a split second can mean the difference between victory and defeat. As a result, intensive research is devoted to creating clothing and equipment that enable athletes to optimize their potential. This trend is sometimes called technological doping because it confers an unfair advantage not unlike using illegal substances, such as steroids, to enhance performance. One instance of possible high-tech doping is the Nike Vaporfly running shoe, the sole of which is constructed to help propel runners forward. Early studies showed that these shoes improved an athlete's running economy – i.e., the energy needed to run at a given pace – by as much as 6.25%. The first runner to cover the marathon distance in less than two hours, a feat generally believed to be humanly impossible in conventional footwear, wore them to do so. In light of the effect of these running shoes, many people have questioned whether athletes should be allowed to wear such shoes in competitions. The World Athletics organization is now reviewing rules regarding the design and construction of running shoes. Another example of high-tech sportswear is competitive swimming gear. Engineers and manufacturers grappled for years with how to counteract drag, a force which impedes progress and has an adverse effect on a swimmer's speed. Swimmers encounter drag predominantly due to the turbulence in the water generated by their movements. The total drag force increases proportionately with speed and inhibits performance. A state-of-the-art bodysuit was launched in 2008 that reduced friction, maintained flexibility, and enhanced buoyancy, letting swimmers float higher in the water. The fabric of the suit was incorporated in a special coating to repel water and decrease resistance. Many competitors adopted it at the Beijing Olympics that year, setting several world records. Whether this was due entirely to the new bodysuits was debatable, but even if the effect on actual times was negligible, the suit appears to have given the wearers a psychological advantage, thus helping them rise to the occasion. Amid the controversy, swimming's governing body moved to ban their use and has since enacted rules to limit how much of a swimmer's body can be covered by their swimwear. The use of technology in sports looks set to remain controversial for as long as there are winners and losers. The question remains as to how far governing bodies should go to restrict the use of technology and where to draw the line between “pure” sports and the unstoppable march of science.
